Case 1 · Oldsmar, Florida · Water treatment
FEB 2021 · THE HEADLINE“Hacker tries to poison city water”
APR 2023 · THE FOLLOW-UPMost likely an employee error

The attribution was wrong.
The findings were right.

  • Shared remote-access password
  • Unsupported Windows 7 workstations
  • Internet-reachable remote desktop
FEB 5, 2021

Lye (NaOH) setpoint jumps from 100 to 11,100 ppm. Operator reverses it.

DAYS LATER

Sheriff's press conference: remote intrusion. Global headlines.

APR 2023

Former city manager: no intrusion — most likely an employee error. FBI: “not able to confirm … a targeted cyber intrusion.”

LESSON

Announce later. Analyze first.

Sources: Industrial Cyber (Apr 2023); ABC Action News I-Team (Apr 2023); Route Fifty (Mar 2023)
  • Everyone in this room has heard Oldsmar as "the hack." It went around the world in 48 hours.
  • [Click] Two years later the former city manager said it was most likely an employee who clicked the wrong thing. Reporting says the FBI found no evidence of an intrusion.
  • [Click] Nuance — don't throw it all out: shared TeamViewer password, Windows 7, internet-facing remote access. The conditions for an attack were real. The attack probably wasn't.
  • [Click] Lesson: the press conference came before the analysis.

The method · Intent or incident?

Four checks before anyone says “hack.”

01 Physics

  • Is the change physically possible for this equipment?
  • Does it match a known failure mode (drift, stiction, bad VFD)?
  • Did the process respond the way the logic says it should?

02 Logs

  • What do the historian and alarm journal say, in what order?
  • Does the HMI audit trail show a write, and from which station?
  • Any gaps, resets, or time jumps?

03 Access

  • Who was logged in, locally or remotely?
  • Any VPN, vendor, or shared-account sessions at that time?
  • Any accounts that should be dead? (Port Hudson)

04 Changes

  • Any open MOC, work order, or logic download?
  • Did maintenance or a vendor touch it this week?
  • Does the running logic match the last known-good backup?

Then call it: equipment · operator · cyber, with ICD 203 likelihood + confidence.

  • This is the actual method behind "do the analysis." Four buckets, any engineer can run them.
  • [Click] Physics first. Most weird events are equipment. Can the process even do this?
  • [Click] Logs. Historian, alarm journal, HMI audit trail. Order of events matters.
  • [Click] Access. Who was on, from where. This is the Port Hudson question nobody asked.
  • [Click] Changes. MOC, work orders, logic downloads. Compare running logic to the known-good backup.
  • [Click] Then make the call, labeled with likelihood and confidence. Tell the students: take a photo of this one.

Borrowed from the intelligence community

Likelihood ≠ Confidence

ICD 203 — U.S. Intelligence Community Directive on Analytic Standards

HOW LIKELY IS IT?

Almost no chance01–05%
Very unlikely05–20%
Unlikely20–45%
Roughly even45–55%
Likely55–80%
Very likely80–95%
Almost certain95–99%

HOW GOOD IS OUR EVIDENCE? — a separate call

LowModerateHigh

“We assess it is likely (55–80%) the setpoint change was operator error — moderate confidence, pending historian and login review.”

  • Two separate questions engineers constantly mash together. How likely? And how good is the evidence behind that?
  • [Click] Confidence is about sources and evidence quality, not about probability.

The MT Floor

Floor: every zone inherits SL 2 on day one. No waiting for a perfect assessment.

Evolve: step a zone up only when consequence demands it:

  • Safety or hazardous-release consequence
  • Environmental or regulatory exposure
  • Crown-jewel production
  • This is my approach. Full per-zone assessments don't scale — at enterprise scale they stall for years.
  • [Click] Every zone inherits SL2 immediately. That's a defensible baseline you can build and audit now.
  • [Click] Then step up where consequence justifies it — SIS, reactors, tank farms, boilers. Spend where the consequences are.
  • Same logic as SIL selection: classify by consequence, inherit requirements, don't reinvent per site.
Cyber-Informed Engineering (DOE / INL)

CIE is FSE with Wi-Fi.

Great in theory

  • Consequence-first, in engineers' language
  • Designs out cyber-enabled hazards
  • Engineered safeguards can't be hacked remotely

Costly in practice

  • Labor-intensive, system by system
  • Needs rare people fluent in both disciplines
  • Best value: capital projects & crown jewels
  • I'm a fan because I'm an actual FSE. A safety function: sense, decide, act.
  • [Click] CIE asks: what if the sensor, the logic, or the network lies? Same hazard thinking, now with Wi-Fi.
  • [Click] Strengths: it's consequence-driven and engineers already think this way. Physical safeguards — relief valves, mechanical interlocks — can't be hacked remotely. They can still be bypassed or neglected, so they need maintenance too.
  • [Click] Weakness: it's expensive and slow to do well. Use it where it pays — new builds and the systems that can hurt people.
  • Hand-off: standards meet real plants.

Legacy vs. modern controllers

Legacy c. 1998 · still running

  • Cleartext protocols, no authentication
  • No signed firmware · vendor may be gone
  • Can fall over from an active scan
  • 20–30 year lifecycle

Modern c. 2024 · new build

  • Secure protocols, role-based access
  • Signed firmware, secure boot
  • Patchable — if you get a window
  • Bigger attack surface: web servers, services

Modern isn't “secure.” It's securable — and it brings new things to configure.

  • Legacy: designed for a trusted, isolated network that hasn't existed for 20 years. Some will crash if you scan them actively. Don't learn that in production.
  • [Click] Modern: real security features — that ship turned off half the time, and bring web servers and services you didn't have before.
  • [Click] Securable, not secure. Somebody still has to configure it.

Four boring things that work

Patch & deprovision

  • Kill accounts the day people leave
  • VPN, shared logins, vendor accounts
  • Patch on the maintenance calendar

Segmentation

Remote access

  • One door, through a jump host
  • MFA, time-boxed, recorded
  • Vendor sessions approved each time

Dumb-but-tough IR

  • One page, not two hundred
  • Operators can run it at 2 a.m.
  • Known-good logic backups
